Toolkit

  • Rectangle: four right angles; opposite sides equal. Perimeter = 2 × (length + width). Area = length × width.
  • Square: four equal sides and four right angles.
  • Diagonals of a square: a diagonal joins opposite corners and is longer than a side. The two diagonals are equal, cross at the centre at right angles, and cut each other in half.
  • Standing on a corner: a square turned so that one diagonal is horizontal and the other is vertical.
  • Upright rectangle: a rectangle whose sides are horizontal and vertical.
  • Perimeter is the length of the boundary of the shape (outside edges only). Area is measured in square units.

Q1

Borrowed perimeter

Achiever

A rectangle measures 9 cm by 5 cm. A square has exactly the same perimeter as this rectangle.

What is the area of the square?

Answer:Units:
Working
Reveal answer and full solution

Final answer: 49 cm²

Key insight

The two shapes share their perimeter, not their area. Use the perimeter to find the square’s side first; only then can its area be found.

Step-by-step reasoning

  1. Perimeter of the rectangle = 9 + 5 + 9 + 5 = 28 cm.
  2. The square has the same perimeter, 28 cm, made of four equal sides.
  3. One side of the square = 28 ÷ 4 = 7 cm.
  4. Area of the square = 7 × 7 = 49 cm².

Calculation

  • Perimeter = 2 × (9 + 5) = 28 cm
  • Side = 28 ÷ 4 = 7 cm
  • Area = 7 × 7 = 49 cm²

Why the method works

A square’s four sides are equal, so its perimeter fixes its side, and its side fixes its area. Notice that the square’s area (49 cm²) is larger than the rectangle’s (45 cm²) although the perimeters are equal.

Common trap

Copying the rectangle’s area (45 cm²), or dividing 28 by 2 instead of 4 — half the perimeter is one length plus one width, not a side of the square.

Q2

Three in a row

Achiever

Three identical squares are placed side by side, each sharing a whole side with its neighbour, to make one rectangle.

The perimeter of this rectangle is 48 cm. Find the area of the rectangle.

Answer:Units:
Working
Reveal answer and full solution

Final answer: 108 cm²

Key insight

Walk around the outside: you travel along exactly 8 sides of the small squares (3 along the top, 3 along the bottom, 1 at each end). The 2 shared sides lie inside the rectangle and are not part of its perimeter.

Step-by-step reasoning

  1. Trace the boundary: top 3 sides + right end 1 side + bottom 3 sides + left end 1 side = 8 square-sides.
  2. These 8 equal sides make 48 cm, so one side = 48 ÷ 8 = 6 cm.
  3. The rectangle is 3 × 6 = 18 cm long and 6 cm wide.
  4. Area = 18 × 6 = 108 cm² (the same as 3 squares of 36 cm²).

Calculation

  • 8 sides = 48 cm → 1 side = 48 ÷ 8 = 6 cm
  • Rectangle = 18 cm × 6 cm
  • Area = 18 × 6 = 108 cm²

Why the method works

Perimeter counts only edges that have the outside on one side. A shared side has a square on both sides of it, so it is inside the rectangle.

Common trap

Counting all 3 × 4 = 12 sides of the three squares. That gives a side of 4 cm and a wrong area of 48 cm².

Q3

One cut, two perimeters

Achiever

A square with sides of 10 cm is cut along one straight line, parallel to one of its sides, into two rectangles A and B.

Rectangle A has a perimeter of 26 cm. What is the perimeter of rectangle B?

Answer:Units:
Working
Reveal answer and full solution

Final answer: 34 cm

Key insight

The cut becomes a side of both pieces. So the two perimeters together are more than the square’s perimeter: they include the cut twice.

Step-by-step reasoning

  1. The cut runs right across the square, so both pieces are 10 cm tall.
  2. Perimeter of A = 10 + 10 + (two widths) = 26 cm, so the two widths of A make 6 cm: A is 3 cm wide.
  3. B is 10 − 3 = 7 cm wide and 10 cm tall.
  4. Perimeter of B = 2 × (7 + 10) = 34 cm.
  5. Second method: A and B together have perimeter 40 (the square) + 2 × 10 (the cut, once for each piece) = 60 cm, so B has 60 − 26 = 34 cm.

Calculation

  • A: 2 × (10 + w) = 26 → 10 + w = 13 → w = 3 cm
  • B: width = 10 − 3 = 7 cm
  • B: 2 × (7 + 10) = 34 cm
  • Check: 40 + 2 × 10 − 26 = 34 cm

Why the method works

Each piece keeps part of the square’s old boundary and gains the cut as a brand-new side. That is why cutting increases the total length of boundary.

Common trap

Thinking the two perimeters add up to the square’s perimeter and answering 40 − 26 = 14 cm. A 10 cm by 4 cm piece would then be needed, which has perimeter 28 cm, not 14 cm.

(b) What are the length and width of the rectangle?

Final answer: (a) 6 cm longer   (b) 12 cm by 3 cm

Key insight

Equal areas do not mean equal perimeters. The rectangle’s sides must satisfy two facts at once: they multiply to 36 and add to 15 (half of 30).

Step-by-step reasoning

  1. Square: side × side = 36, so the side is 6 cm and the perimeter is 4 × 6 = 24 cm.
  2. (a) 30 − 24 = 6 cm. (The rectangle’s own sides are not needed for this part.)
  3. Rectangle: half of its perimeter is one length plus one width, so length + width = 30 ÷ 2 = 15 cm.
  4. Whole-number pairs with product 36: 1 & 36, 2 & 18, 3 & 12, 4 & 9, 6 & 6. Their sums are 37, 20, 15, 13, 12.
  5. (b) Only 3 & 12 adds to 15, so the rectangle is 12 cm by 3 cm.

Calculation

  • Square side = 6 cm (6 × 6 = 36), perimeter = 24 cm
  • (a) 30 − 24 = 6 cm
  • Length + width = 15, length × width = 36 → 12 and 3
  • Check: 2 × (12 + 3) = 30 cm, 12 × 3 = 36 cm²

Why the method works

Half the perimeter equals one length plus one width. Listing the factor pairs of the area and testing their sums finds the only rectangle that matches both facts.

Common trap

Assuming equal areas give equal perimeters and answering 0 cm for (a). The long, thin 12 × 3 rectangle has a much longer boundary than the square.

Q6

Squares off the end

Achiever

From a rectangle 14 cm long and 9 cm wide, the largest possible square is cut off one end. From the rectangle that is left, the largest possible square is cut off again, as shown.

What is the perimeter of the small rectangle that is left over at the end?

Answer:Units:
Working
Reveal answer and full solution

Final answer: 18 cm

Key insight

The largest square that can be cut from a rectangle has a side equal to the rectangle’s shorter side. Each cut subtracts the shorter side from the longer one.

Step-by-step reasoning

  1. The shorter side of the 14 × 9 rectangle is 9 cm, so the first square is 9 cm by 9 cm.
  2. What is left is 14 − 9 = 5 cm wide and still 9 cm long.
  3. The shorter side of this 5 × 9 rectangle is 5 cm, so the second square is 5 cm by 5 cm.
  4. What is left is 5 cm by 9 − 5 = 4 cm.
  5. Perimeter of the left-over rectangle = 2 × (5 + 4) = 18 cm.

Calculation

  • 14 × 9 → cut 9 × 9 → 5 × 9 left
  • 5 × 9 → cut 5 × 5 → 5 × 4 left
  • Perimeter = 2 × (5 + 4) = 18 cm
  • Area check: 81 + 25 + 20 = 126 = 14 × 9

Why the method works

A square cut from a rectangle cannot be wider than the rectangle’s shorter side, and a square of exactly that size fits. So each step removes a square whose side is the current shorter side.

Common trap

Trying to cut a second 9 cm square from a strip that is only 5 cm wide, or forgetting that the strip left after the first cut is 9 cm long (not 14 cm).

Q8

Folded in half

Achiever

A square sheet of paper is folded exactly in half along the dashed line to make a rectangle. The perimeter of the folded rectangle is 30 cm.

What was the area of the original square sheet?

Answer:Units:
Working
Reveal answer and full solution

Final answer: 100 cm²

Key insight

The folded rectangle is one full side of the square long and half a side wide. Its boundary is two full sides plus two half sides — exactly three sides of the square.

Step-by-step reasoning

  1. Folding in half keeps the full width of the square and halves its height.
  2. Perimeter of the rectangle = side + side + half side + half side = 3 sides of the square.
  3. 3 sides = 30 cm, so one side of the square is 30 ÷ 3 = 10 cm.
  4. Area of the original square = 10 × 10 = 100 cm².

Calculation

  • rectangle = side × (half a side)
  • perimeter = 2 sides + 2 half-sides = 3 sides = 30 cm
  • side = 30 ÷ 3 = 10 cm
  • area = 10 × 10 = 100 cm² (check: 2 × (10 + 5) = 30 cm)

Why the method works

Measuring the boundary in “sides of the square” turns the fold into a counting problem. Folding halves the area, but it does not halve the perimeter.

Common trap

Assuming the perimeter halves when the paper is folded: doubling 30 cm to 60 cm gives a side of 15 cm and a wrong area of 225 cm².

Q9

Tiles of unknown size

Achiever

A rectangle is made from 24 identical square tiles arranged in 4 rows of 6 tiles, as shown. The perimeter of the rectangle is 40 cm.

What is the area of the rectangle?

Answer:Units:
Working
Reveal answer and full solution

Final answer: 96 cm²

Key insight

Measure the boundary in tile edges: 6 + 4 + 6 + 4 = 20 tile edges make the 40 cm perimeter.

Step-by-step reasoning

  1. Along the top and bottom there are 6 tile edges each; along each end there are 4. The boundary is 20 tile edges.
  2. 20 tile edges = 40 cm, so one tile edge is 40 ÷ 20 = 2 cm.
  3. Each tile has area 2 × 2 = 4 cm².
  4. Area of the rectangle = 24 × 4 = 96 cm². (Check: it is 12 cm by 8 cm, and 12 × 8 = 96.)

Calculation

  • boundary = 6 + 4 + 6 + 4 = 20 tile edges
  • tile edge = 40 ÷ 20 = 2 cm
  • area = 24 × (2 × 2) = 96 cm²
  • check: 12 × 8 = 96 cm²

Why the method works

Only edges on the outside count towards the perimeter, and each is one tile side. Once the tile side is known, the area follows from the number of tiles or from the rectangle’s length and width.

Common trap

Answering 24 cm² by assuming each tile is 1 cm by 1 cm. A tile is one square centimetre only if its side is 1 cm.

Q10

Three strips make a square

Achiever

A square is cut into three identical rectangular strips by two straight cuts parallel to one of its sides, as shown. Each strip has a perimeter of 24 cm.

What is the area of the square?

Answer:Units:
Working
Reveal answer and full solution

Final answer: 81 cm²

Key insight

Let the width of a strip be 1 part. The square’s side is 3 strip-widths, so each strip is 3 parts long and 1 part wide.

Step-by-step reasoning

  1. Three identical strips fill the square, so a strip is one third as wide as the square and as long as the square’s side.
  2. Call the strip’s width 1 part; its length is then 3 parts.
  3. Perimeter of a strip = 3 + 1 + 3 + 1 = 8 parts = 24 cm, so 1 part = 3 cm.
  4. The square’s side = 3 parts = 9 cm, so its area is 9 × 9 = 81 cm².

Calculation

  • strip = 3 parts × 1 part
  • perimeter = 8 parts = 24 cm → 1 part = 3 cm
  • square side = 9 cm
  • area = 9 × 9 = 81 cm²

Why the method works

Writing every length in the same unit (“parts”) turns the shape’s structure into a simple division. This is the bar-model method used in Olympiad problems.

Common trap

Treating a strip like a square (24 ÷ 4 = 6 cm), or assuming the square’s perimeter is 3 × 24 = 72 cm. The cuts are inside the square, so they are not part of its perimeter.

Seven identical tiles

Advanced Achiever

Seven identical rectangles fit together exactly, with no gaps or overlaps, to form a large rectangle. In the top row two of them lie end to end with their long sides horizontal; in the bottom row five of them stand side by side with their long sides vertical.

The perimeter of the large rectangle is 34 cm. Find the area of one small rectangle.

Answer:Units:
Working
Reveal answer and full solution

Final answer: 10 cm²

Key insight

The top row and the bottom row are both exactly as wide as the large rectangle. So 2 long sides = 5 short sides.

Step-by-step reasoning

  1. Top row width = 2 long sides. Bottom row width = 5 short sides. Both equal the width of the large rectangle, so 2 long sides = 5 short sides.
  2. Use “parts”: if a short side is 2 parts, then 2 long sides are 10 parts, so a long side is 5 parts.
  3. Large rectangle: width = 2 long sides = 10 parts; height = 1 short side + 1 long side = 2 + 5 = 7 parts.
  4. Perimeter = 2 × (10 + 7) = 34 parts = 34 cm, so 1 part = 1 cm.
  5. A small rectangle is 5 cm by 2 cm, so its area is 10 cm².

Calculation

  • 2 × long = 5 × short → short = 2 parts, long = 5 parts
  • large: 10 parts × 7 parts
  • perimeter = 2 × (10 + 7) = 34 parts = 34 cm → 1 part = 1 cm
  • area = 5 × 2 = 10 cm²

Why the method works

Two different routes across the same rectangle must have the same length. That equation fixes the shape of the small rectangle; the perimeter then fixes its size.

Common trap

Guessing that a small rectangle is “twice as long as it is wide” because it looks that way. The drawing only shows how the pieces are arranged; the 2 : 5 relationship must come from the rows.

(b) Find the perimeters of Q and R.

Working
Reveal answer and full solution

Final answer: (a) 28 cm   (b) 26 cm each

Key insight

Equal areas plus a shared side force the other sides to match: P’s height is known (10 cm), and Q and R share a width.

Step-by-step reasoning

  1. Total area = 12 × 10 = 120 cm², so each piece has area 120 ÷ 3 = 40 cm².

Common trap

Splitting the 12 cm length into three equal 4 cm parts for all three pieces, or assuming that equal areas mean equal perimeters.

Reading the side of the big square as “one long + one short” turns the picture into a fact about a single rectangle. Subtracting the hole from the big square counts exactly the four rectangles.

Taking the long side of a rectangle to be the whole side of the large square (10 cm). The long side stops where the next rectangle begins.

Q14

Double trouble

Advanced Achiever

The length and the width of a rectangle are both doubled. The new rectangle has a perimeter of 56 cm and an area of 192 cm².

Find the length and the width of the original rectangle.

Answer:Units:
Working
Reveal answer and full solution

Final answer: Length 8 cm, width 6 cm

Key insight

Doubling both sides doubles the perimeter, but four copies of the original fit inside the new rectangle (2 along × 2 up), so the area is multiplied by 4.

Step-by-step reasoning

  1. Every side doubles, so the perimeter doubles: original perimeter = 56 ÷ 2 = 28 cm, so length + width = 14 cm.
  2. The new rectangle can be split into 2 × 2 = 4 copies of the original, so original area = 192 ÷ 4 = 48 cm².
  3. Find two numbers that add to 14 and multiply to 48: 7 & 7 give 49, 8 & 6 give 48 ✓, 9 & 5 give 45, and the products keep falling as the numbers spread further apart.
  4. The original rectangle is 8 cm long and 6 cm wide.
  5. Check: doubled it is 16 cm by 12 cm: perimeter 2 × (16 + 12) = 56 cm and area 16 × 12 = 192 cm² ✓.

Calculation

  • original perimeter = 56 ÷ 2 = 28 → length + width = 14
  • original area = 192 ÷ 4 = 48
  • 8 + 6 = 14 and 8 × 6 = 48
  • check: 16 × 12 = 192, 2 × (16 + 12) = 56

Why the method works

Perimeter is a length, so it scales like the sides (× 2). Area counts squares in two directions, so it scales twice (× 2 × 2 = × 4). For pairs adding to 14, the product is 49 minus the square of how far each number is from 7, so only 8 and 6 give 48.

Common trap

Halving the area as well as the perimeter. That gives 96 cm², but no two numbers adding to 14 multiply to 96 — a sign that something went wrong.

(a) How long is the cut?
(b) What were the length and width of the original rectangle?

Answer (b):Units:
Working
Reveal answer and full solution

Key insight

The perimeters of the two pieces together are the original perimeter plus the cut twice, because the cut is a side of both pieces.

Step-by-step reasoning

  1. P and Q together: 30 + 40 = 70 cm of boundary.
  2. The original boundary is 54 cm; the extra 70 − 54 = 16 cm is the cut counted twice. (a) The cut is 16 ÷ 2 = 8 cm.
  3. The cut runs straight across between the two long sides, so it is as long as the width: the width is 8 cm.

Cutting never changes the total area, but it always adds the cut twice to the total boundary. Using that fact backwards finds the cut without knowing where it was made.

Common trap

Expecting 30 + 40 to equal 54 and concluding the problem is impossible, or forgetting that a cut parallel to the shorter sides has the same length as the width.
