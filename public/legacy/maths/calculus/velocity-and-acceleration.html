Part I · Intuitive Foundation — the paradox and its resolution

IIntuitive Foundation

I.1 The speedometer paradox, stated exactly

Module 1 ended on a cliff. Average velocity is honest bookkeeping: v̄ = Δx/Δt, the slope of a chord. But your speedometer does something apparently impossible: it displays a velocity now — at a single instant, with Δt = 0. Set Δt = 0 in the definition and Δx = 0 too (nothing has had time to move), so the reading should be the meaningless expression 0/0. Not zero — undefined: 0/0 has no value, because every number q satisfies 0 = q · 0, so division cannot pick one.

Yet physics cannot live without velocity-at-an-instant. The projectile's velocity at the top of its arc, the car's velocity at the moment of impact, the exact instant a rocket's velocity crosses escape speed — all are single-instant questions. The concept is needed; the definition, as it stands, cannot deliver it. This tension is not a nuisance to be waved away. Its honest resolution is the founding act of calculus, and physics — this exact problem, in Newton's hands — is where calculus was born.

I.2 The resolution in one idea: zoom until the motion looks uniform

Here is the physical insight that dissolves the paradox. Take any smooth motion — a car pulling away, a stone falling — and film it. Play one second of the film: the speed visibly changes. Now play one hundredth of a second: the speed barely changes. One millionth: to any instrument you own, the motion within that sliver is indistinguishable from uniform motion — the one kind of motion for which Module 1 already gives an exact, unambiguous velocity.

Reasoning anchor

Every smooth motion, viewed through a strong enough microscope, is uniform motion. Instantaneous velocity is the velocity of the uniform motion the real motion is imitating locally. All of differential calculus is bookkeeping for this single idea.

I.4 Why a second quantity is coming: the two-cars puzzle

The fourth generating question already tells you this module cannot stop at velocity. Two cars alongside each other, with equal velocities, at one instant — yet a minute later, far apart. Position agreed; velocity agreed; the future disagreed. So the pair (x, v) at an instant does not determine what happens next. The missing information is how the velocity itself is changing — one car's needle was climbing, the other's steady. That "rate of change of the rate of change" is acceleration, and the deep reason it completes the story (why x, v, a suffice and no fourth quantity is needed) is a fact about forces, delivered by Newton in Volume 2. Kinematics builds the vocabulary; dynamics will explain why the vocabulary is complete.

Part II · Formal Construction — eight concepts, each built to protocol

IIFormal Construction

II.1 The Limit — the mathematics of "settles on"

Generating question. "Zoom until it looks uniform" is a picture. Physics needs the picture upgraded to a procedure that always gives one definite number and never touches the forbidden 0/0. The limit is that procedure.

The procedure, run by hand. Let a particle move with x(t) = t2 (SI units; you met this curve in Module 1 as the archetypal non-uniform motion). Ask: what is its velocity at t = 3 s? Forbidden to set Δt = 0, we compute the average velocity over the window from 3 to 3 + Δt — for a general, symbolic Δt:

v̄(Δt) = x(3 + Δt) − x(3)Δt = (3 + Δt)2 − 9Δt = 9 + 6Δt + (Δt)2 − 9Δt = Δt (6 + Δt)Δt = 6 + Δt

Watch what the algebra did. The dangerous Δt in the denominator cancelled — legitimately, because Δt is small but not zero, and dividing by a nonzero number is always allowed. After cancellation, the expression 6 + Δt is perfectly tame. Now interrogate it numerically, from both sides of the instant:

Δt (s)+1+0.1+0.01+0.001−0.001−0.01−0.1−1
v̄ = 6 + Δt (m/s)76.16.016.0015.9995.995.95

The averages do not blow up, do not oscillate, do not depend on which side you approach from. They close in on 6 from both directions, and you can force them as close to 6 as you please — within a millionth, within a billionth — merely by taking Δt small enough. That is what "settles on" means, made precise.

Definition · Limit

A quantity f(Δt) has the limit L as Δt → 0, written limΔt→0 f(Δt) = L, if f can be forced to stay as close to L as anyone demands, by keeping Δt sufficiently small (on either side of 0, Δt ≠ 0).

Read every clause: (i) Δt is never set equal to 0 — the limit is about the approach, not the arrival, so 0/0 is never evaluated; (ii) "as close as anyone demands" — the challenger names any tolerance, however savage, and small-enough Δt must meet it; (iii) "either side" — a genuine limit is two-sided; approach from the future (Δt > 0) and from the past (Δt < 0) must agree. (The professional ε-δ formalization of this sentence is Volume 0, Module 7 material; nothing in mechanics up to and including IPhO needs more than the working version stated here — but you should know a sharper version exists.)

Non-examples — because a definition is only understood when you can watch it fail. (i) f(Δt) = 1/Δt has no limit as Δt → 0: it explodes past every bound. (ii) f(Δt) = |Δt|/Δt equals +1 for every positive Δt and −1 for every negative Δt: each side settles, but on different numbers, so the two-sided limit does not exist. Case (ii) is not a curiosity — it is exactly what happens to velocity at a sharp bounce (see II.2, kinks), which is why the definition insists on both sides.

Derived · The three limit laws

Suppose f → L and g → M as Δt → 0. Then:

(Sum) f + g → L + M     (Scale) c f → c L     (Product) f g → L M

Why each is true (working justification): write f = L + (small error e1), g = M + (small error e2), where "small" means shrinkable below any tolerance. Then f + g = (L + M) + (e1 + e2): the sum of two shrinkable errors is shrinkable. And f g = L M + L e2 + M e1 + e1e2: each correction term contains at least one shrinkable factor multiplied by something bounded, so all three corrections shrink. Errors add, scale, and multiply the way small numbers do — that is the entire content. These three laws are the license behind every derivative rule in II.3; nothing there will be pulled from thin air.

Reasoning anchor

A limit is a destination, not a value at the destination. Cancel first (Δt ≠ 0 makes it legal), then let Δt → 0. The whole art is postponing zero until it is harmless.

II.2 Instantaneous Velocity — the derivative is born

Definition · Instantaneous velocity

The instantaneous velocity of a particle at time t is the limit of its average velocity over a window at t, as the window shrinks to nothing:

v(t)  =  limΔt→0 x(t + Δt) − x(t)Δt  ≡  dxdt

The right-hand symbol — read "dee-x by dee-t", called the derivative of x with respect to t — is pure notation for the limit on its left. The letter d (reserved since Module 1) means "Δ, pushed to the limit": an infinitesimal change, one already inside the limiting process. Signed like Δx was: v > 0 means motion toward +x at this instant. Units m/s; dimensions [v] = LT−1 — a limit of ratios inherits the ratio's dimensions. Every value v(t) at every t assembles into a new function of time: differentiation eats the function x(t) and produces the function v(t).

Worked from the definition — velocity at a general instant for x = t2, not just at t = 3:

(t + Δt)2 − t2Δt = 2t·Δt + (Δt)2Δt = 2t + Δt  ⟶  v(t) = 2t

Check it against the hand computation: v(3) = 6 m/s ✓. One symbolic computation has replaced infinitely many numerical tables — that is the leverage the derivative buys.

Geometry: the chord pivots into the tangent

Module 1 established: average velocity = slope of the chord joining two points of the x–t graph. Now watch the limit happen geometrically. Hold the first point A fixed at (t0, x(t0)) and slide the second point B along the curve toward A. The chord AB swings, and as B closes in, the chord tightens onto one final resting line — the line that grazes the curve at A.

Definition · Tangent line

The tangent to a curve at a point A is the line through A whose slope is the limit of chord slopes as the chord's second endpoint approaches A. Note the honesty of this: we did not "already know" what a tangent was and then discover its slope is the derivative — the derivative is what defines the tangent. (School geometry's "line touching a circle at one point" is a special case that does not generalize; a tangent may perfectly well re-cross the curve elsewhere.)

Reasoning anchor

Average = chord. Instantaneous = tangent. On any x–t graph, "how fast at this instant" is a question about the tangent's slope at that point, and nothing else. Half of all graph questions surrender to this sentence alone.

Theory: what the definition demands of x(t) — continuity and smoothness

The limit does not exist for free; the position function must cooperate. Two requirements, both with physical meaning:

(a) Continuity — no teleporting. A particle cannot jump instantaneously from one position to another: it must pass through the intermediate positions. Graphically, x(t) is an unbroken curve. This is a physical input (Module 1's boundary table already flagged it), and it is mathematically necessary too: if x jumped at t, then Δx/Δt would blow up as Δt → 0 and no velocity would exist there.

(b) Smoothness — no perfect corners. Suppose a ball hits a wall and its graph has a sharp kink: slope +5 just before, −5 just after. The one-sided limits disagree (+5 from the past, −5 from the future — precisely non-example (ii) of II.1), so velocity at the kink instant is undefined. Reality's escape: real collisions are not instantaneous; slowed a millionfold, the "kink" is a very tight but smooth turn, and velocity exists throughout. The perfect kink is an idealization — a useful one, formalized as the impulse approximation in Volume 3 — and the price of using it is one instant with no defined velocity. Knowing exactly what an idealization costs is part of owning it.

It frequently behaves like a fraction (that good behavior is why Leibniz designed it so, and it can be made fully rigorous), but you may not casually rip dx and dt apart until the legitimate techniques for doing so are built (Module 3 and Volume 0, Module 8). II.3 The Differentiation Toolkit — every rule earned

Generating question. Physics hands us position functions constantly — t2, 5t3 − 2t, ut + ½at2. Running the raw limit each time is honest but slow. We now run it once per pattern, harvest the rules, and never need the raw computation again — while always retaining the ability to reproduce it (Block B will make sure).

Derived · Rule 1 — constants

x(t) = c (parked at position c). Then x(t + Δt) − x(t) = c − c = 0 for every window, so every average is 0 and the limit is 0.

ddt(c) = 0   —  no change, no rate of change.
Derived · Rule 2 — straight lines

x(t) = ct: the difference quotient is [c(t+Δt) − ct]/Δt = c exactly, for every window — no limit even needed. So d(ct)/dt = c. This is Module 1's uniform motion re-derived: constant slope, at every instant, equal to the average over any interval.

Derived · Rule 3 — the power rule, by counting

For x = t3: expand (t + Δt)3 = t3 + 3t2Δt + 3t(Δt)2 + (Δt)3. Then

(t+Δt)3 − t3Δt = 3t2 + 3t·Δt + (Δt)2  ⟶  3t2

Now the general power tn, by a counting argument that shows why the rule is what it is. Write (t + Δt)n as a product of n identical brackets: (t + Δt)(t + Δt)···(t + Δt). Multiplying out means: from each bracket, pick either t or Δt, multiply your picks, and sum over all possible pick-patterns. Sort the results by how many Δt's were picked:

  • Zero Δt's: pick t from all n brackets — one way — giving tn.
  • Exactly one Δt: choose which bracket donates it — n ways — each giving tn−1Δt. Total: n tn−1 Δt.
  • Two or more Δt's: every such term carries a factor (Δt)2 at least. Bundle them all as (Δt)2 × (bounded stuff).
(t + Δt)n = tn + n tn−1 Δt + (Δt)2·(bounded)

Subtract tn, divide by Δt, let Δt → 0: the middle term survives untouched, the bundle dies with its surviving factor of Δt.

ddt tn = n tn−1

Interpretation of the two visible moves: the exponent drops by one because one bracket "spent" its t on the change; the factor n appears because any of the n brackets could have been the spender. The rule is combinatorics, not magic. (This expansion-to-first-order is also your first sighting of a Taylor series — the systematic version arrives in Volume 0, Module 10.)

Derived · Rule 4 — linearity (sum & constant multiple)

Δ-algebra from Module 1: Δ(x + y) = Δx + Δy and Δ(cx) = cΔx (both are one-line checks from "final minus initial"). Divide each identity by Δt, apply the Sum and Scale limit laws of II.1:

ddt(x + y) = dxdt + dydt      ddt(c x) = c dxdt

Physical reading: if a total displacement is the sum of two contributions, the total rate is the sum of the rates. Rates inherit the bookkeeping of the changes they came from.

The toolkit's reach. Rules 1–4 differentiate any polynomial in t, term by term — and polynomials cover every motion this module and the next several need. Worked specimen, every step visible once (and never again):

x = 4t3 − 6t2 + 5t − 7  ⟹  v = dxdt = 4(3t2) − 6(2t) + 5(1) − 0 = 12t2 − 12t + 5

What the toolkit does not yet contain — and why that is deliberate. No product rule, quotient rule, chain rule, and no derivatives of sin, cos, exponentials. Under this curriculum's founding law, mathematics enters when physics demands it: those tools are demanded by circular motion, oscillations, and decay (Modules 7, Volume 5, Volume 9), and each will be built at its point of need. What you have now is complete for everything up to and including projectile motion.

Dimensional theory of the derivative

A derivative's dimensions are always [numerator]/[denominator]: [dx/dt] = LT−1, and later [dv/dt] = LT−2 — because a limit of ratios keeps the ratio's dimensions. Practical weapon: in any derived formula, each differentiation with respect to t must strip exactly one power of T. If your algebra produces v = 2t2 from x = t2, dimensions convict it instantly. Free, permanent error detection — use it after every differentiation until it is reflex.

II.4 Instantaneous Speed — a theorem, not a definition

Generating question. Module 1 left a deliberate asymmetry: average speed and |average velocity| are genuinely different numbers whenever motion reverses. Does the same gap infect the instantaneous quantities? Remarkably — no. And the reason why is a small, perfect illustration of what "instantaneous" buys you.

Theorem · instantaneous speed = |instantaneous velocity|

Claim. For motion with continuous velocity, the limit of (distance in window)/(window) equals |v(t)| at every instant.

Argument. The gap between average speed and |average velocity| has exactly one source: reversals inside the window (Module 1's master inequality, with its equality condition "no reversal"). Now shrink the window around an instant where v(t) ≠ 0. Velocity is continuous, so sufficiently near t it keeps the sign it has at t — a small enough window contains no reversal at all. Within such windows, distance = |Δx| exactly, so the two averages coincide exactly, so their limits coincide: speed(t) = |v(t)|. At an instant where v(t) = 0, continuity forces |v| to be arbitrarily small throughout small windows; the distance covered in a window of width Δt is then at most (largest |v| in the window)·Δt, so distance/Δt → 0 = |v(t)|. ∎

Physical soul of the proof: zoomed in far enough, the motion has no time to turn around. Reversal is a finite-interval phenomenon; the instant is too small to hide one.

II.5 Acceleration — the derivative machine, applied twice

Generating question. The two-cars puzzle (I.4): position and velocity at an instant do not determine the future; the missing quantity is the rate at which velocity itself changes. We already own a machine that converts "quantity" into "rate of that quantity." Feed it velocity.

Definition · Acceleration
ā = ΔvΔt  (average)      a(t) = limΔt→0 ΔvΔt = dvdt = d2xdt2  (instantaneous)

Signed (a 1-D vector), like everything built from Δx. Units m/s² — read it physically: "metres per second, of velocity, gained per second." An acceleration of 3 m/s² means the velocity reading climbs by 3 m/s during each second (exactly, if a is constant; instantaneously, in general). Structurally this section is II.2 verbatim with v in the role of x — same limit, same chord-to-tangent geometry on the v–t graph: average acceleration = chord slope of v–t; instantaneous acceleration = tangent slope of v–t. You are not learning a second mechanism; you are re-running the only mechanism there is.

The second-derivative notation, decoded. d2x/dt2 abbreviates ddt(dxdt) — the operation d/dt applied twice to x. The superscripts are operation-counting, not squaring: nothing is multiplied by itself. (The placement — d2 up top, dt2 below — is Leibniz's bookkeeping so that dimensions read off correctly: L above, T2 below, giving LT−2 ✓.) The full chain, run on our specimen:

x = t3  ⟶d/dt  v = 3t2  ⟶d/dt  a = 6t  ⟶d/dt  dadt = 6  (the "jerk")

Does the ladder ever end? In principle you may differentiate forever (the third rate, jerk, is real engineering: passengers feel sudden changes of acceleration — a train easing its brakes on gently is minimizing jerk). But physics will grant acceleration a privileged position: Newton's second law (Volume 2) fixes a directly from forces, which — together with starting position and velocity — determines the entire future. That is why kinematics stops building vocabulary here: x, v, a is not an arbitrary stopping point but the complete set the laws of dynamics will consume. The puzzle of I.4 will be closed, not shelved.

Derived · Acceleration is the curvature of the x–t graph

a is the rate of change of v, and v is the slope of x–t. Therefore a > 0 means the slope of x–t is increasing — reading left to right, the graph turns steadily upward (concave up, a cup ∪). a < 0: slope decreasing, graph bends downward (a cap ∩). a = 0 over an interval: slope frozen, graph straight. Value → slope → bend. On an x–t graph: height is position, steepness is velocity, curvature is acceleration. Three layers of information stacked in one picture — always ask which layer a question is interrogating.

II.6 The Sign Algebra of Motion — the deadliest misconception, dismantled

Generating question. Velocity and acceleration are both signed. Two signs give four combinations. What does each combination feel like — and in particular, when is the object speeding up versus slowing down?

The derivation — from the definition, not from vibes. "Speeding up" means the speed |v| is increasing. Track |v| over a small window: v changes by approximately a·Δt. If a has the same sign as v, the change pushes v further from zero — |v| grows. If a has the opposite sign, the change pulls v toward zero — |v| shrinks. The whole classification compresses into one product:

v·a > 0  ⟹  speeding up       v·a < 0  ⟹  slowing down       (signs, not sizes, decide)
Misconception — the deadliest in kinematics

Tempting intuition: "Negative acceleration means slowing down; deceleration and negative acceleration are the same thing."

Hidden assumption: that the object is moving in the +direction — usually because everyday examples (a car braking on a rightward road) quietly are.

Why it fails: the sign of a states which way the velocity is being pushed, not whether the speed shrinks. A ball moving in the −direction with a < 0 is being pushed deeper into negative velocity: it speeds up.

More accurate model: read the pair (v, a). Same sign — faster; opposite signs — slower. Better still, banish the word "deceleration" from your written solutions: it is frame- and direction-relative slang. State signs.

Why it matters in problems: a ball thrown upward has a = −g at every instant of flight (up-positive convention) — yet on the way down it is speeding up under that same negative acceleration. Every vertical-motion problem in II.8 is a live minefield for this error, and JEE/NSEP set the mines deliberately.

Reasoning anchor

Never read the sign of a alone; read it against the sign of v. Aligned = speeding up. Opposed = slowing. That is the entire law.

II.7 Constant Acceleration — the five laws, each one earned

Generating question. The simplest non-trivial acceleration is a constant one — and nature, remarkably, serves it up in the single most common motion on Earth (free fall, next section). What exact predictions does "a = constant" force?

The model and its notation

Assumption (the model): a is the same number at every instant of the interval considered. Consequence, immediate and load-bearing: since instantaneous and average acceleration then coincide over any window, the v–t graph is a straight line of slope a — and every derivation below secretly leans on that straightness. Standard symbols (all signed components along a declared axis; the vector versions v⃗ = u⃗ + a⃗t etc. reduce to these in 1-D): u = velocity at t = 0; v = velocity at time t; a = the constant acceleration; s = displacement (not distance!) accumulated between 0 and t.

Law 1 — velocity grows linearly

Derived

Constant a means ā = a over the interval [0, t]: (v − u)/t = a. Rearrange:

v = u + at

Reading: start with u; each second deposits a more. Straight line on the v–t plane: intercept u, slope a. Dimensions: [at] = LT−2·T = LT−1 ✓.

Law 2 — displacement: three independent derivations

One law, three proofs — because each proof exposes a different face of the physics, and an Olympiad solver needs all three faces.

Derivation A · by area (the geometric face)

First, the key sub-idea — why displacement is the area under a v–t graph at all. For uniform motion, displacement = v × t: numerically the area of a rectangle of height v and width t. For changing velocity, slice time into slivers so thin that within each the motion is effectively uniform (the zoom principle of Part I — this is its first industrial application): each sliver contributes ≈ v·Δt of displacement, which is the area of a thin rectangle under the graph. Summing slivers sums the rectangles; refining the slicing turns the staircase of rectangles into the exact region under the curve. (Signed: slivers where v < 0 contribute negative displacement — area below the axis counts negative. The full, general theory of this sum-of-slivers — integration — is Module 3's opening act; constant acceleration needs only the straight-line case, computed now.)

For constant a, the region under the straight v–t line from 0 to t is a rectangle plus a triangle:

Derivation A, concluded
s = ut + ½at2

Term-by-term physics: ut is the displacement of the coasting twin (Module 1's fictitious uniform mover at the initial velocity); ½at2 is the accumulating correction the acceleration adds. Kill the acceleration (a = 0) and only the coast survives — the formula degrades gracefully into Module 1's uniform law, exactly as any correct generalization must.

Derivation B · by symmetry pairing (the averaging face)

Because v(t) is a straight line, its values pair off around the midpoint of the interval. Take any instant t′ and its mirror t − t′ (equally far from the two ends). Sum their velocities:

[u + at′] + [u + a(t − t′)] = 2u + at = u + (u + at) = u + v  — the same for every pair.

Every mirror-pair of instants averages to (u + v)/2 — early slowness and late swiftness offset exactly, sliver by paired sliver. So the time-average of the velocity is the plain midpoint value, and displacement = (time-average of v) × t:

s = u + v2 · t     (Law 3, arriving as a by-product)

Boundary, in red ink: the innocent-looking mean (u + v)/2 is a privilege of the straight v–t line. Curve the line (varying a) and the mirror-pairs no longer sum to a constant — the shortcut dies. Module 1's harmonic-mean trap taught you to distrust naive averaging; here, at last, is the one situation where the naive average is exactly right, and the precise reason why.

Derivation C · by guess-and-verify (the calculus face)

The most modern proof, and a template you will reuse for years. We seek a function x(t) whose derivative is the known velocity u + at. Propose x(t) = x0 + ut + ½at2 and verify with the toolkit:

dxdt = 0 + u(1) + ½a(2t) = u + at ✓     and differentiating again: dvdt = a ✓

The candidate reproduces the required velocity at every instant and starts at x0. But is it the only motion that does? Yes — by a principle worth naming:

The no-change theorem. If a quantity's instantaneous rate of change is zero at every instant of an interval, the quantity is constant on that interval. Physically: a car whose speedometer reads exactly zero for the whole trip has not moved. Apply it: if two motions share the same v(t) throughout, their difference has zero rate at every instant, hence never changes; if they also share a starting position, the difference is zero forever — the motions are identical. (The theorem is physically self-evident and true; its airtight mathematical proof needs the Mean Value Theorem, delivered in Volume 0, Module 8. Flagged, not fudged.)

What this face reveals: "find the motion given the acceleration" is our first differential equation — a law prescribing rates, solved by exhibiting a function with those rates. Newton's mechanics will consist of exactly such problems (Volume 0, Module 9 makes this systematic). The ½ in ½at2, mysterious to memorizers, is transparent here: it is precisely the coefficient that the power rule's factor of 2 will cancel.

Law 4 — eliminating time

Derived

Problems often supply speeds and distances but neither give nor want the time. Solve Law 1 for it — t = (v − u)/a — and substitute into Law 3:

s = u + v2 · v − ua = v2 − u22a   ⟹   v2 = u2 + 2as

Note the algebra's gift: (u+v)(v−u) = v2 − u2, the difference of squares. And note the physics' whisper: a relation between squared speeds and displacement, with time expelled. In Volume 3 this exact shape — multiplied through by ½m — becomes the work–energy theorem. Law 4 is energy conservation traveling incognito; remember its face.

Law 5 — displacement during the n-th second

Derived

The displacement during second number n (from t = n−1 to t = n) is a difference of two Law-2 evaluations:

sn = [un + ½an2] − [u(n−1) + ½a(n−1)2] = u + a2(2n − 1)

The dimensional puzzle, resolved — do not skip this. As printed, both terms look like velocities (m/s), yet sn is a displacement in metres. The formula is written in SI shorthand: a factor of (1 s) multiplies u and a factor of (1 s²) multiplies the second term — invisible because both equal 1 in SI. Fully honest form: sn = u·(1 s) + ½a(2n−1)·(1 s²), dimensions clean. Two consequences: (i) the formula is only valid with SI inputs — feed it km/h and it lies; (ii) sn is "displacement over a one-second window," not a rate. Exam setters know students memorize this law blind; the dimensional trap is set accordingly.

The boundary that governs all five — and a live counterexample

Boundary · constant a only

Every derivation above leaned on the straightness of the v–t line: the trapezoid (A), the mirror-pairing (B), the specific quadratic guess (C). If a varies, all five laws are void. Watch one fail. Take x = t3 (so v = 3t2, a = 6t — manifestly not constant), and ask for v at t = 2 s. Truth: v = 3(4) = 12 m/s. Now misuse Law 1 with u = 0: which "a" do you plug in? Using a(2) = 12 gives v = 24 — wrong by ×2. Using a(0) = 0 gives v = 0 — absurd. Only the true time-average of a (here 6 m/s², the value at t = 1) rescues Law 1, and in general you do not know that average in advance. Discipline: before touching any of the five laws, write the sentence "assuming constant acceleration because …" — and have a because. In INPhO's descriptive format, that sentence is also worth marks.

Consistency web — the free error-detector

The five laws are not independent: any two of {Law 1, Law 2} generate the rest. A constant-acceleration problem is therefore over-determined — after solving with one law, a second law re-derives your answer from different inputs. Two routes agreeing is powerful evidence of correctness; disagreeing is certain proof of error. Institute the habit now: every constant-a answer gets one independent cross-check. It costs twenty seconds and catches the majority of algebra slips before an examiner does.

II.8 Free Fall — nature's constant-acceleration laboratory

The empirical pillar. Near the Earth's surface, with air resistance removed, every body — stone or feather, heavy or light — falls with the same constant downward acceleration:

g ≈ 9.8 m/s²  (use 10 m/s² where a problem says so)   —  the same for all bodies, directed vertically down.

Three separations, in the Module-1 spirit of keeping strands apart. Observed: in vacuum, a feather and a hammer released together land together — demonstrated on the Moon by Apollo 15, and in evacuated drop-towers on Earth; measured g varies slightly with location (≈9.78 at the equator, ≈9.83 at the poles, smaller at altitude). Idealized: "free fall" assumes air resistance negligible and g uniform over the trajectory — excellent for a dense ball over tens of metres; poor for a feather in air or a rocket over hundreds of kilometres (Part VI). Not yet explained: why all masses share one acceleration is one of the deepest facts in physics — gravitational force is proportional to exactly the mass that resists acceleration. Newton takes the first pass at it (Volumes 2 and 4); Einstein rebuilt physics around it. Kinematics records the fact and computes with it.

The entire technique of free-fall problems is sign discipline. Declare an axis; then g enters as a component: up-positive ⟹ a = −g; down-positive ⟹ a = +g. The symbol g itself always denotes the positive magnitude 9.8 m/s². Both conventions give identical physics (Concept 7 of Module 1 guarantees it); mixing them mid-solution gives garbage. Block C, Problem C2 runs one problem through both conventions as proof and vaccination.

The anatomy of a vertical throw — every classic result derived

Throw a ball straight up with speed u; up-positive, origin at the hand, a = −g. The two governing laws: v(t) = u − gt and y(t) = ut − ½gt2.

  • Apex time. The top of the flight is where the ball stops rising: v = 0 ⟹ tup = u/g.
  • Maximum height. Law 4 with v = 0: 0 = u2 − 2gH ⟹ H = u2/2g. Read the scaling: double the throw speed, quadruple the height — the square in Law 4 at work.
  • Total flight time. Return to launch height: y = 0 ⟹ t(u − ½gt) = 0 ⟹ t = 0 (launch — the equation politely returns the moment you already knew) or T = 2u/g = 2tup: descent takes exactly as long as ascent.
  • Return velocity. v(T) = u − g(2u/g) = −u: same speed, opposite direction.
Derived · The symmetry theorems of vertical flight

Theorem S1 (mirror in time). The height at equal time-offsets before and after the apex is the same: y(tup + τ) = y(tup − τ). Proof. Expand with gtup = u:

y(tup ± τ) = u(tup ± τ) − ½g(tup ± τ)2 = [utup − ½gtup2] ± τ(u − gtup) − ½gτ2 = H − ½gτ2

The ± term carries the factor (u − gtup) = v(tup) = 0 — it vanishes because the apex is where velocity vanishes. The flight is a palindrome centered on its apex.

Theorem S2 (equal speeds at equal heights). From Law 4, v2 = u2 − 2gy: the speed at height y depends only on y, not on the direction of travel or the time of visit. Passing 15 m on the way up and on the way down: same speed, opposite velocity.

Why the symmetry exists at all — the structural reason, which generalizes: the acceleration is constant, hence indifferent to the direction of time. Run the film of the descent backwards and it satisfies the same law as the ascent (velocities flip sign; the acceleration, a second derivative, does not — two sign flips cancel). Any motion governed by constant a inherits this time-reversal symmetry; air resistance, which opposes the current velocity, breaks it — and real trajectories in air are indeed asymmetric (slower coming down, shorter on the far side). Symmetry present ⟹ idealization holding; symmetry broken ⟹ look for the velocity-dependent intruder. That inference pattern is pure Olympiad.

Misconception · "at the top, a = 0"

Tempting intuition: the ball hangs motionless at the apex, so "nothing is happening" — acceleration must be zero.

Hidden assumption: that acceleration is a property of moving, so no motion ⟹ no acceleration.

Why it fails: acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, and at the apex velocity is changing as briskly as ever — from small-positive to small-negative, straight through zero. On the graph above: the line crosses the axis without the slightest change of slope.

More accurate model: v = 0 is a statement about the value; a ≠ 0 is a statement about the trend. A zero with a nonzero trend is a zero just passing through.

Why it matters: if a were truly 0 at the apex, the velocity would stop changing while at 0 — the ball would hover forever. Balls do not hover; the misconception refutes itself. Direct MCQ bait in NSEP/JEE, and the same confusion re-attacks later at pendulum turning points and spring extremes (Volume 5).

Derived · Galileo's odd-number rule — the fingerprint of constant acceleration

From rest (u = 0), Law 5 gives sn = ½a(2n − 1)·(1 s²): successive-second displacements proportional to 1, 3, 5, 7, … And for any u, subtracting consecutive Law-5 values: sn+1 − sn = a·(1 s²) — successive equal-time displacements always form an arithmetic progression with common difference a·(Δt)2 (Module 1, Block C5, now proved in full generality and for any window Δt). This is a diagnostic instrument: given strobe data, take differences of differences — constant ⟹ constant acceleration, and the constant divided by (Δt)2 is a. It is also history: Galileo, lacking clocks fast enough for free fall, rolled balls down gentle inclines — diluting gravity to a measurable trickle while preserving its constancy — and found the odd numbers. Slowing a phenomenon down without changing its structure remains a first-rank experimental idea.

Reasoning anchors · II.7–II.8

1. Constant a ⟺ straight v–t line — and all five laws are just that line's geometry (slope, trapezoid area, mirror symmetry).

2. ½at2 is a triangle whose base and height both grow with t; the ½ is the triangle's half, pre-paid for the power rule's 2.

3. v2 = u2 + 2as is the time-free law — reach for it the moment a problem talks speeds-and-distances and never mentions the clock.

4. Free fall is a palindrome about its apex — equal times, equal speeds at equal heights — because constant acceleration cannot tell time's arrow. Air resistance can, and breaks it.

The general polynomial motion — and the coefficient dictionary

Quadratics in time — the solver's contract

Constant-acceleration position laws are quadratic in t, so questions "when is the particle at y = …?" produce at2 + bt + c = 0 with up to two roots. The physics of the root count: two real roots — the particle visits that position twice (up and down through the same height); one (repeated) root — it barely grazes the position (the apex case); no real root (negative discriminant) — the event never happens (the ball never reaches that height). And a root at negative t is not garbage: it answers a legitimate question about the extrapolated past — "when would the particle have been there, had this motion always been running?" Block F turns this into a full technique. The discriminant is a physical instrument, not an algebra chore.

VAlternative Viewpoints

Viewpoint 1 — laws as evolution rules. Rearrange perspective on II.7's Derivation C: "the acceleration is a" is an instruction — from wherever you are, with whatever velocity you have, change your velocity at rate a — and the motion is what results from obeying it forever. A rule prescribing rates plus a starting state generating the whole future: that is a differential equation, the native language of every fundamental law (Newton's, Maxwell's, Schrödinger's). What it reveals: physics laws are local instructions, not global formulas; the formulas are consequences. What it conceals: for most rules no tidy formula exists at all, and one solves numerically — which is viewpoint 2.

Viewpoint 2 — the discrete world: how machines differentiate. A sensor owns no limits, only samples. From samples x(t) at spacing h, two estimators of v(t):

forward: x(t+h) − x(t)h      central: x(t+h) − x(t−h)2h

Test both on x = t2 at t = 3 with a coarse step h = 1: forward gives (16 − 9)/1 = 7 (error 1); central gives (16 − 4)/2 = 6, exactly. Not luck: expand the central quotient symbolically — [(t+h)2 − (t−h)2]/2h = 4th/2h = 2t, the error terms cancel by symmetry: the forward window's overshoot (curve bending up ahead) is exactly repaid by the backward window's undershoot. Straddling the instant symmetrically buys an order of accuracy for free — which is why lab software, phone sensors, and every physics simulation you will ever write use central differences. What this viewpoint reveals: the derivative is the idealization; the strobe is the reality; and symmetry improves even arithmetic. What it conceals: the clean theorems (no-change, mirror pairing) that only the continuous idealization supports.

Viewpoint 3 — time reversal as an analytical instrument. Run any motion's film backwards: every Δt flips sign, so v = dx/dt flips sign — but a, a rate of a rate, flips twice and comes back unchanged. Consequence: any motion with velocity-independent acceleration, run backwards, is again a legal motion under the same law — that is the true engine behind II.8's palindrome theorems, now stated frame-free. And its contrapositive is a detector: whenever a real trajectory is visibly time-asymmetric (a shuttlecock's steep descent), some velocity-dependent influence (drag) is present. Symmetry arguments deliver conclusions without computation; their cost is that they never deliver the numbers — for those, back to the laws.

VIIMisconceptions and Diagnostic Contrasts

The two deadliest — "negative a means slowing" and "a = 0 at the apex" — were dismantled at their source (II.6, II.8). Three more, protocol format:

Misconception · "(u+v)/2 always works"

Tempting intuition: average velocity is just the average of the starting and ending velocities.

Hidden assumption: that velocity changed uniformly — a straight v–t line.

Why it fails: the mirror-pairing proof (II.7, Derivation B) required straightness. Counterexample: x = t3 over [0, 2]: endpoint velocities 0 and 12 average to 6, but true v̄ = Δx/Δt = 8/2 = 4. The curve spends most of the interval slow; the naive mean ignores time-weighting — Module 1's harmonic-mean lesson in new clothes.

More accurate model: (u+v)/2 is a theorem about constant a, not a definition of average velocity. The definition is, forever, Δx/Δt.

Why it matters: examiners bait it with variable-a graph problems; and the correct special case is genuinely useful, so you must carry both the tool and its license.

Misconception · "dx/dt is a fraction — cancel the d's, split them freely"

Tempting intuition: it is written as a fraction; fractions split.

Hidden assumption: that dx and dt are numbers with independent existence.

Why it fails: they are the two halves of a single limit symbol; "cancelling" them proves anything, including falsehoods, because 0/0 lurks beneath.

More accurate model: treat dx/dt as one atomic operator applied to x. Fraction-like manipulations (separating dx = v dt) become legitimate in Module 3, where each such move is backed by a theorem about limits — the notation is designed so that legal moves look natural; your job until then is knowing which moves are backed.

Why it matters: premature fraction-surgery is the single largest source of silent calculus errors in Olympiad scripts — wrong by a sign or a factor, undetectably, deep inside a long solution.

Misconception · "constant speed ⟹ zero acceleration" (true here, false soon — flagged honestly)

Tempting intuition: speed not changing ⟹ nothing about the motion is changing.

Hidden assumption: that velocity has only a size. In one dimension, that is almost true — and indeed in 1-D the claim is a theorem: constant |v| with continuous v forces v itself constant (to flip sign it would have to pass through 0, violating |v| = const), hence a = 0.

Why it will fail: in two dimensions velocity is an arrow, and an arrow can turn at constant length. A car rounding a bend at a steady 60 km/h is accelerating — its velocity's direction changes — and something must push it sideways (you feel that push). Module 7 quantifies it (v2/r, toward the center).

More accurate model: acceleration is the rate of change of the velocity vector — magnitude or direction, either counts.

Why it matters: flagged now so the 1-D theorem never fossilizes into a general "law" that Module 7 must painfully break.

Bonus · Galileo's thought experiment — why "heavier falls faster" refutes itself

Suppose heavier bodies fell faster. Tie a light stone to a heavy one with a string and drop the pair. The light stone, "falling slower," should drag on the heavy one — so the tied pair falls slower than the heavy stone alone. But the pair is also a single object heavier than the heavy stone — so it should fall faster. One assumption, two contradictory predictions: the assumption is dead, by pure logic, before any experiment. The only escape is that fall rate is independent of mass — exactly what the vacuum experiments confirm. Keep this argument: deriving physics from a contradiction is an Olympiad-grade weapon, and this is its most famous deployment.

Diagnostic contrasts — say each difference aloud in one sentence, no formulas: average vs instantaneous velocity (chord vs tangent); velocity vs acceleration (how fast position changes vs how fast velocity changes); v = 0 vs a = 0 (momentarily at rest vs velocity frozen); slowing down vs negative acceleration (opposed signs vs one sign alone); Δ vs d (finite change vs change inside a limit); curvature vs slope (bend vs steepness).

What the expert sees

The missing-variable selector. Each constant-a law omits exactly one quantity: Law 1 (v = u + at) never mentions s; Law 2 (s = ut + ½at2) never mentions v; Law 3 (s = (u+v)t/2) never mentions a; Law 4 (v2 = u2 + 2as) never mentions t. The expert's first move on any constant-a problem: identify the quantity that is neither given nor asked, and reach for the law that omits it. One decision replaces four attempts.

Scaling reflexes (from rest): s ∝ t2, v ∝ t, v ∝ √s, fall time ∝ √(h/g), impact speed ∝ √(gh). Half of all "what if the height doubles / gravity is one-sixth" questions are answered by these proportionalities in one line, before — or instead of — any computation. Block F, part (d) trains it.

Quadratics in t return two roots; INPhO-style grading rewards stating why a root is accepted or rejected ("t = 2 − √2 s < 1 s contradicts 'the last second' existing") — and APhO problems sometimes make the "rejected" root the physical answer (the virtual-history reading, Block F part (a)). Destructive shortcuts: any of the five laws under undeclared variable a; (u+v)/2 on curved v–t; plugging g with a sign that contradicts your declared axis; treating s as distance when reversals occurred.

Part X · Training Blocks — concept locks to Olympiad build-up

XTraining Blocks

Block A — Concept Locks (attempt cold before reading each answer)

A1

Can an object have zero velocity and nonzero acceleration at the same instant? Zero acceleration and nonzero velocity? Give a physical example of each.

Answer

Both are possible, and both matter. v = 0, a ≠ 0: the ball at its apex — velocity momentarily zero, acceleration −g throughout; equivalently, any turning point of any motion. a = 0, v ≠ 0: uniform motion — a cruising train. The two quantities answer different questions (value vs trend) and constrain each other at an instant not at all.

A2

In one-dimensional motion with continuous velocity, can the speed be constant while the velocity changes?

Answer

No — and proving it is the point. Constant speed means v = +c or −c at every instant. To switch between them, the continuous function v(t) must pass through every intermediate value, including 0 — but |0| ≠ c, contradicting constant speed. So v never switches: velocity is constant, a = 0. File the honesty flag from Part VII: the proof leans on velocity being a signed number; in two dimensions an arrow can turn at constant length, and this comfortable theorem dies (Module 7).

A3

At some instant a particle has x > 0, v < 0, a > 0. Describe, in one sentence each: where it is, what it is doing, and what is happening to what it is doing.

Answer

It sits on the positive side of the origin; it is moving toward the origin (−direction); and it is slowing, because v·a < 0 — the acceleration opposes the motion. (Whether it will stop before reaching the origin depends on magnitudes, not signs — a fact Law 4 can settle. Note what the exercise trains: three independent signs, three independent facts, zero interference.)

A4

Two cars' x–t graphs cross at instant t1. At t1, do the cars share position? Velocity? Now suppose instead the graphs touch tangentially at t1 — what changes?

Answer

Crossing: same height ⟹ same position (they meet); slopes generally differ ⟹ different velocities — one is overtaking. Tangency: same height and same slope ⟹ same position and same velocity at that instant — a "grazing" encounter, the geometric signature of just-barely-catching (the limiting case in pursuit problems, used in D4). Curvatures may still differ: same place, same velocity, different accelerations — the two-cars puzzle of Part I, drawn.

A5

A particle moves with constant acceleration a > 0 and initial velocity u < 0. Describe the entire future of the motion, unprompted.

Answer

Phase 1: v < 0 with a > 0 — opposed signs, slowing while moving in the −direction. At t = −u/a (positive, since u < 0): momentary rest, acceleration intact. Phase 2: v > 0 with a > 0 — aligned, speeding up in the +direction forever. Net picture: the particle penetrates some maximum distance −u2/2a into the −direction (Law 4 with v = 0), turns, and returns through its start at speed |u| (S2's logic). One constant acceleration, a complete round-trip story told by signs alone.

Block B — Derivation and Reconstruction

B1

Derive d(t4)/dt from the raw limit, using the counting argument — no citation of the power rule.

Reconstruction

(t+Δt)4: four brackets. All-t pick: t4. Exactly one Δt: 4 choices of donor bracket ⟹ 4t3Δt. Everything else carries (Δt)2. Subtract t4, divide by Δt, send Δt → 0: survivors 4t3. (If your reconstruction did not count — if the 4 appeared by pattern-matching — redo it: the counting is the understanding.)

B2

Rebuild the (u+v)/2 average-velocity theorem by the mirror-pairing argument, and state exactly where the argument uses constancy of a.

Reconstruction

Pair instants t′ and t−t′; sum of paired velocities = [u+at′] + [u+a(t−t′)] = u + v, independent of t′ — because v(t) is linear in t, i.e., because a is constant; that substitution is the exact point of use. Every pair averaging to the same number forces the time-average to that number; displacement = average × duration.

B3

Derive v2 = u2 + 2as twice: (i) by eliminating t; (ii) directly from the v–t trapezoid, without ever writing t = (v−u)/a as a separate step.

Reconstruction

(i) II.7 verbatim: s = ½(u+v)t with t = (v−u)/a gives s = (v2−u2)/2a. (ii) The trapezoid's area = (mean height)(base) = ½(u+v) · (v−u)/a read as one geometric statement: base expressed via slope, base = rise/slope. Same algebra, but the second route shows the law as a pure property of the straight line — height-squared difference = 2 × slope × area. Two faces, one truth; owning both is the point.

B4

Derive the n-th-second law sn = u + ½a(2n−1) and then write its dimensionally honest form. Under what input units does the shorthand silently lie?

Reconstruction

sn = s(n) − s(n−1); expand both with Law 2; the difference of squares n2 − (n−1)2 = 2n−1 delivers the form. Honest version: sn = u·(1 s) + ½a(2n−1)·(1 s²). The shorthand lies whenever u or a arrive in non-SI units (km/h is the classic ambush) — the hidden (1 s) factors only equal 1 in seconds-based units.

Block C — Standard Application (fully worked to the Solution Standard)

C1 · direct, with the consistency web

A car starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at 2 m/s² for 5 s. Find its final velocity and displacement, and cross-check by an independent law.

Solution

Model & frame. Particle; +x along motion, origin and clock at the start; constant a stated by the problem ("uniformly"). Knowns. u = 0, a = +2 m/s², t = 5 s. Selection. v unknown wanted: Law 1 (omits s). s unknown wanted: Law 2 (omits v).

Execution. v = 0 + 2(5) = 10 m/s. s = 0 + ½(2)(25) = 25 m.

Verification (independent law). Law 4, which used neither result's route: v2 = 0 + 2(2)(25) = 100 ⟹ v = 10 ✓. Law 3 as a second check: s = ½(0+10)(5) = 25 ✓. Dimensions and plausibility: 10 m/s = 36 km/h after 5 s of brisk but ordinary acceleration ✓.

Interpretation. The displacement (25 m) is exactly half of the coasting-at-final-speed value (50 m) — from rest, the triangle is the whole area, and a triangle is half its rectangle. That "half" generalizes: from rest, distance always lags the naive final-speed estimate by exactly ×½.

C3 · pursuit: constant velocity vs constant acceleration

A speeder passes a stationary police car at a steady 30 m/s. At that instant the police car starts from rest with constant acceleration 3 m/s². Find (a) when and where the police catch the speeder, (b) the maximum lead the speeder attains and when, (c) the police car's speed at the catch — and explain why that speed is exactly double the speeder's, always.

Solution

Frame. +x along the road, origin and t = 0 at the passing event. Position functions: xS = 30t; xP = ½(3)t2 = 1.5t2.

(a) Catch = meeting (Module 1's move). 30t = 1.5t2 ⟹ t(1.5t − 30) = 0 ⟹ t = 0 (the passing itself — the equation reports the meeting we already knew, a good sign) or t = 20 s, at x = 30(20) = 600 m. Check via the police: 1.5(400) = 600 ✓.

(b) Maximum lead. The gap G(t) = 30t − 1.5t2 grows while the speeder is faster and shrinks once the police are faster — so it peaks at the instant of equal velocities: 3t = 30 ⟹ t = 10 s, G = 300 − 150 = 150 m. Confirm with the derivative: dG/dt = 30 − 3t = 0 at t = 10 ✓ — the calculus and the physical argument are the same argument, which is the lesson.

(c) Catch speed. vP(20) = 3(20) = 60 m/s = 2 × 30. Why always double, from rest: catching means equal displacements, ½at2 = uSt ⟹ at = 2uS — and at is the pursuer's velocity. Equivalently by averages: to cover the same ground in the same time from rest, the pursuer's average (0 + v)/2 must equal the target's constant uS. A tiny theorem, worth carrying.

Verification. Dimensions ✓; limiting sense: smaller a ⟹ later catch (t = 2uS/a) ✓; 60 m/s (216 km/h) says real pursuits end by radio, not by this model — reading a model's answer back onto reality is part of the solution.

C4 · two bodies, one gravity — the g-cancellation

Ball A is dropped from the top of a 100 m tower at the instant ball B is thrown straight up from its base at 25 m/s (g = 10 m/s²). When and where do they meet, and is the meeting valid (both still in flight)?

Solution

Frame. Up = +, origin at the ground, t = 0 at release. yA = 100 − 5t2; yB = 25t − 5t2.

Meeting. 100 − 5t2 = 25t − 5t2. The quadratic terms cancel identically: 100 = 25t ⟹ t = 4 s, at y = 100 − 80 = 20 m.

Why the cancellation is physics, not luck. Gravity edits both trajectories by the identical term −½gt2 (same g for all bodies — the empirical pillar of II.8), so the separation yA − yB = 100 − 25t evolves as if gravity did not exist: the gap closes at a constant 25 m/s. Seen from ball A, ball B approaches uniformly. This is the doorway to Module 5 (relative motion), opened one crack: common accelerations vanish from relative descriptions.

Validity audit — required, not optional. A lands when yA = 0: t = √20 ≈ 4.47 s > 4 ✓. B: apex at 2.5 s, back to ground at 5 s > 4 ✓ (at t = 4, vB = 25 − 40 = −15 m/s — B is met on its way down). Meeting valid.

Verification. Same height by both formulas: yB(4) = 100 − 80 = 20 ✓. Limiting case: throw speed ↓ toward 100/tfall ≈ 22.4 m/s and the "meeting" slides to the ground itself — consistent with the closing-speed picture (slower closing, later meeting, lower altitude).

C5 · the last-second problem — root rejection as physics

A stone falls from rest and covers half of its total height in the final second of its fall (g = 10 m/s²). Find the total time of fall and the height.

Solution

Setup. Down = +, from rest: h = 5T2. Distance in the last second = h − 5(T−1)2. Condition: h − 5(T−1)2 = h/2, i.e. 5(T−1)2 = ½·5T2.

Execution. 2(T−1)2 = T2 ⟹ T2 − 4T + 2 = 0 ⟹ T = 2 ± √2.

Root adjudication — the heart of the problem. T = 2 − √2 ≈ 0.59 s is a genuine root of the algebra but not of the physics: a fall shorter than 1 s has no "final second," so the modelling equation was never valid there. State this; do not silently discard. Accept T = 2 + √2 ≈ 3.41 s, whence h = 5(2+√2)2 = 30 + 20√2 ≈ 58.3 m.

Verification. Distance in last second: h − 5(1+√2)2 = (30+20√2) − (15+10√2) = 15 + 10√2 = h/2 ✓. Plausibility: ~58 m ≈ an 18-storey building; 3.4 s of fall ✓.

Error analysis. Two standard wrecks: taking "half the height in the last second" to mean the last second equals half the time (it does not — later seconds cover more ground, the odd-number rule); and accepting 2 − √2 because "it is positive." Positivity is not validity; the constraint T ≥ 1 s came from the problem's own words.

Block D — Transfer Problems (the governing idea is not announced)

D1 · measure your own reaction time — a real experiment

A friend holds a 30 cm ruler vertically; you position your fingers at the 0 cm mark without touching. The friend releases without warning; you catch it at the 20 cm mark. What is your reaction time? Then perform the experiment tonight and report honestly.

Solution

The ruler free-falls from rest through d = 0.20 m during your reaction interval: d = ½gt2 ⟹ t = √(2d/g) = √(0.04) = 0.2 s — a typical human value. The ruler is a clock: distance stores time via the square-root law, so the scale readings 5, 11.25, 20, 31.25 cm mark 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.25 s. Note the compression: equal time-ticks crowd together near the top and spread lower down — you are seeing s ∝ t2. This module's single-source text can derive that law; only your own hand can feel 0.2 s. Do the experiment.

D2 · why doubling speed quadruples the braking distance

A car's brakes provide a fixed maximum deceleration magnitude a. Show that the braking distance grows as the square of the speed, and explain to a non-physicist why 60 km/h is four times as dangerous as 30 km/h in required stopping room — not twice.

Solution

Time is neither given nor wanted ⟹ Law 4 (the missing-variable selector at work): 0 = v2 − 2as ⟹ s = v2/2a ∝ v2. Double v: quadruple s. The non-physicist explanation, two compounding factors: at double speed you must shed twice the velocity, and during every moment of shedding you are travelling twice as fast — twice the velocity to lose × twice the ground per second of losing = four times the ground. (Real stopping adds reaction distance v treaction, linear in v, on top — total v tr + v2/2a. Road-safety charts are this formula, printed.)

D3 · model selection: the sprinter

A sprinter accelerates uniformly at 2.5 m/s² up to a top speed of 10 m/s, then holds that speed. Model her 100 m race time. Which single modelling decision is doing the most work?

Solution

Phase 1 (constant a): reach 10 m/s in t1 = 10/2.5 = 4 s, covering s1 = ½(2.5)(16) = 20 m. Phase 2 (uniform): remaining 80 m at 10 m/s takes 8 s. Total: 12.0 s — a fit club athlete, sensible. The load-bearing decision is the piecewise structure: no single constant-a model spans a race (a runner cannot accelerate at 2.5 m/s² throughout — she would finish at 22 m/s, beyond human). Splitting reality at the point where one idealization hands over to another is the first genuinely Olympiad-flavoured modelling skill; every phase gets its own law, joined by matching v and x at the seam.

D4 · bounding before computing: the two trains

Two trains approach each other on one track at 20 m/s and 30 m/s. When 625 m apart, both drivers brake, each with deceleration magnitude 1 m/s². (a) Do they collide? (b) What common braking magnitude would just avoid collision?

Solution

(a) Bound, don't simulate. Each train, braking to rest, needs s = v2/2a: train 1 needs 400/2 = 200 m; train 2 needs 900/2 = 450 m. Combined ground consumed even in the best case (both fully stopping): 650 m > 625 m available ⟹ they collide — no timeline computation needed. This is C-grade arithmetic wrapped around an A-grade move: comparing total demand against total supply settles the binary question before any dynamics.

(b) The just-avoid condition. Grazing safety = combined stopping demand equals the gap: (400 + 900)/2a = 625 ⟹ a = 1300/1250 = 1.04 m/s². Verification: at 1.04, demands are 192.3 + 432.7 = 625.0 ✓ — they halt nose-to-nose. Interpretation: the margin between disaster and safety is 4% of braking power; railway signalling exists because this margin is intolerable.

D5 · the fingerprint test

A particle's position (from the origin, at rest at t = 0… or is it?) is observed to double every second: x = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 m at t = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 s. Could its acceleration be constant?

Solution

Apply Galileo's diagnostic (II.8): successive-second displacements are 1, 2, 4, 8 m — a geometric progression, ratio 2. Constant acceleration demands an arithmetic progression with common difference a·(1 s²): differences of the displacements are 1, 2, 4 — not constant. Verdict: not constant acceleration, and no choice of u, a can be tortured into fitting (three conditions already over-determine the two parameters and contradict). Deeper: doubling is exponential growth, whose rate is proportional to the current value — a new kind of law, the one governing radioactive decay and charging capacitors, awaiting the exponential function (built at its point of need, Volumes 7 and 9). Recognizing "this is outside my current model class" is itself the skill.

Block E — Synthesis with Module 1

E1 · the jogger's ghost — when must average = instantaneous?

Module 1's jogger: +3 m/s for 100 s, rest for 50 s, −2 m/s for 50 s; average velocity +1 m/s. Is there any instant at which her instantaneous velocity equals +1 m/s? What does the answer teach about idealizations?

Solution

Her instantaneous velocity only ever takes the values {+3, 0, −2} — never +1. No such instant exists. Yet intuition (and a deep theorem — the Mean Value Theorem, Volume 0) insists a smooth traveller must at some moment move at exactly her average pace. The escape: her motion is not smooth — the piecewise model has kinks, instants of undefined velocity where the speed "jumps" from 3 to 0 to −2 without visiting intermediate values. A real jogger decelerates through +1 m/s during some fraction of a second; the idealization amputated those fractions and, with them, the theorem's guarantee. Price tags on idealizations (II.2's kink discussion) are real, and this is one being paid. When Volume 0 delivers the MVT, you will already know exactly which hypothesis (smoothness) it cannot live without.

E2 · the traffic-light chase

At the instant a light turns green, a car starts from rest with a = 2 m/s², just as a truck rolls past through the intersection at a steady 10 m/s. Find when and where the car catches the truck, the truck's maximum lead, and the car's speed at the catch — then predict the last answer before computing, from C3's theorem.

E3 · full autopsy of a polynomial motion

A particle moves with x(t) = t3 − 6t2 + 9t (SI). Over [0, 4 s], find every instant of momentary rest, classify each (true reversal or not), and compute displacement and total distance. This deliberately fuses Module 1's distance/displacement split with this module's calculus.

Solution

Differentiate. v = 3t2 − 12t + 9 = 3(t−1)(t−3): rest at t = 1 and t = 3. Sign chart of v: + on (0,1), − on (1,3), + on (3,4) — both are true reversals (sign change). Cross-check with a = 6t − 12: a(1) = −6 ≠ 0 and a(3) = +6 ≠ 0 — genuine turning points, not flat pauses.

Waypoints. x(0) = 0; x(1) = 1 − 6 + 9 = 4; x(3) = 27 − 54 + 27 = 0; x(4) = 64 − 96 + 36 = 4.

Totals. Displacement = x(4) − x(0) = +4 m. Distance = |0→4| + |4→0| + |0→4| = 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 m — computable only by splitting at the reversals; integrating good intentions over the whole interval at once is exactly the error Module 1's master inequality warns of (12 ≥ |4| ✓, strict because reversals exist ✓).

Interpretation. Out to 4 m, all the way back to the start, out to 4 m again — a there-and-back-and-there journey encoded in one cubic. The sign chart of v is the itinerary; everything else is evaluation.

F1 · The balloon and the stone

A hot-air balloon rises with constant velocity 5 m/s. At the moment it is 60 m above the ground, a stone is released (not thrown) from it. Take g = 10 m/s², air resistance negligible.

(a) Find when the stone hits the ground — and give the physical meaning of the quadratic's other root.
(b) Find the stone's maximum height and the total distance it travels.
(c) Find the impact speed by two independent routes.
(d) The same release happens on the Moon (g/6 ≈ 1.67 m/s²) from a hovering (stationary) balloon at the same 60 m. Without solving any new quadratic, determine how fall time and impact speed scale, then evaluate.
(e) Write the examination-compressed solution.

Full solution

Interpretation & the embedded trap. "Released" is not "dropped from rest relative to the ground": at the instant of release the stone shares the balloon's velocity — initial velocity +5 m/s upward. (Why released objects inherit their carrier's velocity is a dynamics fact — inertia, Volume 2; kinematics takes the initial condition as given, but the exam counts on you forgetting it.) After release, only gravity acts: free fall with u = +5.

Frame. Up = +, origin at the ground, t = 0 at release. y(t) = 60 + 5t − 5t2; v(t) = 5 − 10t.

(a) Ground: 60 + 5t − 5t2 = 0 ⟹ t2 − t − 12 = 0 ⟹ (t − 4)(t + 3) = 0 ⟹ t = 4 s. The root t = −3 s is the virtual history: the parabola we solved describes an eternal free-fall motion, of which the stone's actual flight is only the t ≥ 0 part. Had that eternal motion always been running, it would have left the ground 3 s before release, rising — and indeed, launched upward from the ground at v(−3) = 5 + 30 = 35 m/s, a projectile reaches exactly 60 m with exactly +5 m/s three seconds later (check: 35(3) − 5(9) = 105 − 45 = 60 ✓). The rejected root is a consistency oracle, not garbage — and APhO problems have been built entirely around such extrapolated-past readings.

(b) Apex where v = 0: t = 0.5 s, rising a further ½u2/g… directly: rise = u2/2g = 25/20 = 1.25 m ⟹ maximum height 61.25 m. Distance ≠ |displacement| because the path reverses (Module 1 discipline): up 1.25 m, then down 61.25 m ⟹ total distance = 62.5 m, versus |Δy| = 60 m ✓ (strict inequality, one reversal).

(c) Impact speed, twice. Route 1 (Law 1): v(4) = 5 − 40 = −35 ⟹ speed 35 m/s. Route 2 (Law 4, time-free): v2 = 52 + 2(−10)(−60) = 25 + 1200 = 1225 ⟹ 35 m/s ✓. Independent routes agree — the consistency web closing. (Notice also: 35 m/s is exactly v(−3) with sign flipped — S2's equal-speeds-at-equal-heights, stretched across the virtual history. Everything in this problem is one parabola wearing four disguises.)

(d) Scaling, not solving. With a hovering balloon, u = 0, and only two quantities govern: h and g. From rest: t = √(2h/g) and vimpact = √(2gh). Send g → g/6 at fixed h: fall time scales by √6 ≈ 2.45× and impact speed by 1/√6 ≈ 0.41×. Earth reference values: √12 ≈ 3.46 s and √1200 ≈ 34.6 m/s; Moon: t ≈ 8.5 s, v ≈ 14.1 m/s. (Direct check on the Moon: √(2·60/1.667) = √72 ≈ 8.49 s ✓.) One line of proportionality replaced a full re-solve — this reflex, generalized, is Volume 10's dimensional-analysis machinery.

(e) Exam compression — the mark-earning skeleton (≈ 6–8 min). (1) "Stone inherits balloon velocity: u = +5 m/s up; thereafter free fall, constant a = −g." [assumption + model marks] (2) Quadratic, factored; t = 4 s; one clause rejecting −3 s with reason. (3) Apex +1.25 m ⟹ 61.25 m; distance 62.5 m (state the reversal). (4) Impact 35 m/s via Law 4 [fewer steps than Law 1 — but citing both, two lines, buys verification credit cheaply]. Stone 1 is dropped from a high cliff; stone 2 is dropped from the same point exactly 1 s later. (a) Show that once both are falling, their separation grows linearly in time, and find its growth rate. (b) Explain the linearity in one sentence using an idea from C4. (c) At what time after stone 1's release is the gap 25 m? (g = 10 m/s².)

Hint 1 · observation

Write both positions from one clock: stone 1 falls for t, stone 2 for (t − 1), valid for t ≥ 1.

Hint 2 · representation

Gap = 5t2 − 5(t − 1)2. Expand before concluding anything.

Hint 3 · governing principle

The t2 terms cancel — the same cancellation as C4, and for the same physical reason.

Hint 4 · strategy

In stone 1's frame, stone 2's acceleration is g − g = 0: relative motion is uniform, at whatever relative velocity existed when both were airborne. What was stone 1's speed at t = 1?

Hint 5 · key equation

Gap = 5(2t − 1) = 10t − 5: rate 10 m/s = g × (1 s). Set equal to 25.

Checkpoint

Growth rate 10 m/s; gap = 25 m at t = 3 s (from stone 1's release).
